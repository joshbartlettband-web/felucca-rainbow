# Amethyst's London

Notes on the music Amethyst comes from, written so Josh and kipatchi can build from the real thing. Every date and
name here has a source at the bottom. Where the sources disagree, it says so.

---

## Kingston, then London

It starts with a mistake. In Kingston in 1968, Ruddy Redwood took a dubplate cut at Treasure Isle to a dance. The
engineer had left the vocal off. Redwood played the instrumental anyway, and the crowd loved it. One account says he
played it for over half an hour, bringing it back again and again. That's the rewind, and probably dub too.

Jamaica's sound systems go back to Kingston in the 1940s: a selector, a stack of speakers, records nobody else had.
People from the Caribbean brought them to Britain in the 1960s and '70s. By the late '80s, the kids who grew up
around those systems in London were also going to raves. Jungle is what came out when the two met: reggae's bass,
MCs, dubplates and rewinds, over breakbeats played twice as fast.

Even the name came from Kingston, though nobody agrees exactly how. The best-known story is that people in Arnett
Gardens called their estate the concrete jungle and themselves junglists. Rebel MC sampled a sound-system tape
shouting out "alla di junglists," and the word crossed the Atlantic. MC Navigator of Kool FM told Simon Reynolds he
first heard it in 1991, in that sample. Other people tell it differently.

## A timeline, by place

1988. Rage starts on Thursdays at Heaven, a club in the arches under Charing Cross station. Fabio and Grooverider
play breakbeats, house, hardcore and techno side by side. Sources say it ran until 1993 or 1995. Nobody called it
jungle yet.

1991. Kool FM goes on air on November 28, at 94.5, started by Eastman and Smurff. It broadcasts about 70 hours a
week from the roofs of the Nightingale Estate in Hackney. You recorded your show, climbed to the roof and played the
tape through a homemade transmitter. One founder remembers using a fishing rod to cast cables from one tower block
to the next.

1993. Dreamscape puts out a blow-molded pack of five cassettes, recorded live at one of its raves. Tape packs
become the way you hear the music: six, eight, twelve tapes from one night, each a DJ and an MC.

1994. Rinse FM starts in September, a jungle pirate run by Geeneus and Slimzee, for a while out of Slimzee's house.
Goldie, Kemistry and Storm start Metalheadz. Renegade's "Terrorist" (Ray Keith, on Moving Shadow) takes one note of a
1988 Detroit bassline and builds jungle's darkest sound on it.

1995. The Metalheadz Sunday Sessions open in July at the Blue Note, a club in a forgotten corner of Hoxton Square.
Eddie Otchere is the photographer. Across town, LTJ Bukem's Speed starts at the Velvet Rooms, smoother and more West End;
it later becomes Fabio's Swerve. Goldie's *Timeless* comes out and goes to number seven.

1996. Ed Rush and Trace, with the engineer Nico at No U-Turn, start calling their hard, cold sound techstep. Two
years later Ed Rush and Optical's *Wormhole* takes it into neurofunk.

1998. Stevie Hyper D dies on July 5. He'd invented double-time MCing, twice the words in the same bars.

1999. Fabric opens in Farringdon in October. Its first public night is drum and bass, and Fridays stay that way:
FABRICLIVE, with DJ Hype's Playaz there almost from the start.

2000. Fabio puts out a compilation on his Creative Source label and calls it *Liquid Funk*. The name sticks.

2001. Dillinja and Lemon D bring the Valve sound system to Fabric in May: 52 subwoofers of their own design, 96 kW.
They built it because club systems couldn't play their bass.

2004 to 2005. Grime and dubstep come up out of garage, a lot of it on Rinse. At DMZ in Brixton, the decks sit at
the crowd's level so people can shout for rewinds, or hold up five fingers to ask for one.

2006. Double O and Mantra start Rupture, a jungle night. It finds its home at Corsica Studios in Elephant and Castle
about two years later and stays until Corsica closes.

2007. Burial's *Untrue*. He said the tracks were about coming home from a night out: in a minicab, on a night bus,
walking across London late. The cover is his own drawing of a figure out in the rain.

2008 and 2009. Turnmills closes, then The End.

2010. Rinse gets a legal license in June, after a three-year campaign.

## How it sounded, and why

The breaks were sampled on Akai samplers, the S950 and the S1000 (1988), and sequenced on an Atari ST. Producers
mostly sped the breaks up, pitch and all, so they'd fit; the S1000 could time-stretch, and its rough, chopped kind
of stretching became part of the sound. The Amen, Think and the others were cut into slices and played back in new
orders. That's the black keys.

The Reese bass is Kevin Saunderson's, from "Just Want Another Chance" by Reese (Detroit, 1988). He made it on a Casio
CZ; sources disagree on the model. Renegade sampled one note of it for "Terrorist" in 1994, and after that it was
everywhere: Alex Reece's "Pulp Friction" (Metalheadz, 1995), DJ Trace's "Sonar" (1998). Melodee's CZ-1 engine is the
same kind of synth. Amethyst's Reese should be a CZ patch.

The hoover started as a joke. Eric Persing, who wrote the Roland Alpha Juno's presets in 1985, made one called "What
The." In 1991, Joey Beltram and Mundo Muzique's "Mentasm" (as Second Phase) turned it into the sound of hardcore.
It's a sawtooth with its pulse width wobbling, which Melodee's PROPHET can do.

The bass weight is the sound systems'. Dub's bass, sub you feel more than hear, and space in the mix for echo.

Dubplates were the currency. Music House, which Chris Hanson of Black Slate started at his home in Finsbury Park in
the 1980s, moved to Holloway Road, and nearly every jungle DJ on the circuit cut plates there. Paul Chue ran it; his
son Leon worked there from 1995 until he died in late 2020. A VIP was a "variation in production," a version of a
track only one DJ had, sometimes for years. A special named the DJ or the sound system on the record itself.

## The people at the front

The MC is half of it. MCs came from Jamaica with the sound systems, and in jungle they ran the night: Stevie Hyper D,
then Skibadee, MC GQ on the AWOL tapes. GQ remembers being the first to stop a whole mix and bring it back: "Stop the
mix! Stop the mix! Rewind the whole mix." The calls are "pull up," "wheel it up," "come again," "rewind."

A rewind is the crowd's. It happens when a tune is too good to let go: they bawl for it, the MC calls it, and the DJ
spins the record back by hand, the needle hurtling across the grooves, or stops it with the start button for a cleaner
one. On the pirates, listeners rang the studio to ask. Telepathy's parties at Marshgate Lane claim they brought the
rewind to raves; the AWOL crowd at the Paradise Club say the same about GQ.

## How it looked

Flyers were sci-fi paintings. Junior Tomlin, "the Salvador Dalí of rave," airbrushed them after the space art of
Chris Foss and Jim Burns; his first music job was Renegade Soundwave's *Space Gladiator* in 1989. Pez did Helter
Skelter, World Dance and Rain Dance, and says Tomlin was the only other person doing it for a living.

Then the tapes: a clamshell box with the flyer's art on it and the line-up on the spine. Then Eddie Otchere's photos
of the Blue Note. Then, ten years on, Burial's pencil figure in the rain.

The city in it is council estates and roofs with aerials, the West End at closing time, Hoxton before it was Hoxton,
the night bus home.

## The feeling Amethyst is after

Future noir is already a London thing, and it has names. Mark Fisher called Burial's music hauntology: the sound of
futures that were promised and didn't arrive, with "disappointed lovers on night buses" in it (*Ghosts of My Life*,
2014). Simon Reynolds called the whole line from hardcore to jungle to garage to grime to dubstep the hardcore
continuum, each style growing out of the last.

Amethyst can run along that line. A London where the raves went quiet and the moss came up through the concrete, and
the old sound systems still play underneath it. Vinyl crackle, rain, reverb, a Reese from 1988, a break from 1969.

## Getting it right

This is mostly Black British music, made by people who grew up around Jamaican sound systems in London, and by
several accounts its history has been told without them before. Amethyst is being made at least partly a long way from
Hackney. Some things follow from that.

1. Credit the history in the firmware itself. Each stage gets a few lines of sleeve notes: the year, the place, the
   people. They're in section 4 of AMETHYST.md.
2. No made-up patois on the screen. The calls MCs really use ("rewind," "come again," "pull up," "big up") are fine,
   written plainly. A real MC's voice, recorded with their say-so and their name on it, would be better than any text.
3. A venue's name can caption a photo of the venue, as it does on each stage. Its logo, its flyers and the labels'
   marks stay out, and the people who ran it are named in the sleeve notes.
4. Ask someone from the scene to look at it before it goes out. kipatchi may know who.
5. Use real voices and photos only with permission, credited. A photo changed by an AI says so, and keeps its
   photographer's name.

## To read, watch and hear

Books: Simon Reynolds, *Energy Flash* (1998). Brian Belle-Fortune, *All Crews* (1999), the scene in its own words.
Martin James, *State of Bass* (1997). Mark Fisher, *Ghosts of My Life* (2014). Junior Tomlin, *Flyer & Cover Art*
(Velocity Press). *We Are Rupture* (Velocity Press), on Rupture's 20 years. The Metalheadz book from its 30th year.

Films: *RAGE: The Club Night That Inspired a Generation* (2019). *HYPER*, on Stevie Hyper D. Rinse FM's documentary
series. The half-hour film on Music House.

Records, in order: Reese, "Just Want Another Chance" (1988). Second Phase, "Mentasm" (1991). Renegade, "Terrorist"
(1994). Goldie, *Timeless* (1995). Ed Rush and Optical, *Wormhole* (1998). *Liquid Funk* (Creative Source, 2000).
Burial, *Untrue* (2007). And the tape packs: Stamina MC has put hundreds of them online.
